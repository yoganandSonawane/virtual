Theory

Hardenability, the Jominy test principle, and material science background

💡 What is Hardenability?

Hardenability is the ability of a steel to be hardened to a given depth by quenching. It is a measure of how deep the hardness penetrates into the cross-section of a steel piece when subjected to a given quench. Hardenability should not be confused with hardness — hardness is the resistance to surface penetration, while hardenability describes the depth to which hardening occurs.

Key Distinction: High hardenability steel develops martensite throughout its cross-section; low hardenability steel only hardens at the surface, with a soft core beneath.
🧪 Jominy End-Quench Test Principle

The Jominy test (ASTM A255 / ISO 642) provides a standardized way to compare hardenability. A standard cylindrical specimen (25.4 mm dia × 102 mm long) is heated to the austenitizing temperature, then rapidly transferred to a fixture where only the bottom end is quenched by a controlled jet of water. The rest of the bar cools in still air.

This creates a gradient of cooling rates along the bar length — very fast at the quenched end (high hardness if martensite forms), and very slow at the opposite end (lower hardness, ferrite/pearlite structure). After quenching, flat surfaces are ground along the bar and Rockwell hardness (HRC) is measured at 1.6 mm (1/16-inch) intervals from the quenched end.

Water jet specification: Free-water height from orifice = 63.5 mm, orifice diameter = 12.5 mm. Flow must produce an umbrella pattern when the specimen is in place.
🔬 Microstructural Transformations
  • Martensite — forms at high cooling rates (quenched end). Very hard, brittle, body-centred tetragonal structure.
  • Bainite — intermediate cooling rate. Mixture of ferrite and fine cementite. Moderate hardness.
  • Pearlite — slow cooling (far end). Lamellar ferrite + cementite. Lower hardness.
  • Ferrite — very slow cooling. Soft, ductile BCC iron phase.
⚗️ Factors Affecting Hardenability
  • Carbon content — higher carbon increases maximum attainable hardness
  • Alloying elements — Mn, Cr, Mo, Ni retard pearlite/bainite transformation, increasing hardenability
  • Grain size — coarser austenite grain increases hardenability
  • Austenitizing temperature — higher temp dissolves more carbides, increasing hardenability
  • Cooling rate — controlled by distance from quenched end
📐 Specimen Dimensions
Diameter25.4 mm (1 inch) — standardized to ensure uniform cooling rate distribution.
Length102 mm (4 inches) — sufficient to cover the full range of cooling rates in one test.
Collar / ShoulderA small shoulder near one end allows the specimen to hang in the Jominy fixture without touching water during transfer.
🔴
Quenched End

Maximum cooling rate ~700°C/s. Nearly 100% martensite. Highest hardness. Represents shallow hardenability zone.

🟡
Mid Section

Mixed martensite + bainite. Intermediate hardness. Cooling rate ~10–50°C/s depending on steel alloy.

🔵
Air-cooled End

Slowest cooling (~2°C/s). Pearlite/ferrite structure. Lowest hardness. Hardness ≈ normalized condition.

Procedure

Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for Jominy End-Quench Test

🔧 Pre-operational Checks
  1. 1
    Prepare Standard Specimen

    Prepare a cylindrical specimen of size diameter 25.4 mm and length 102 mm with a shoulder at one end (as per ASTM A255 standard). Ensure the specimen is cleaned and free of scale or oxide layers.

  2. 2
    Heat Specimen in Furnace

    Heat the specimen in a furnace up to austenitic temperature (typically 870°C for most carbon steels) for the prescribed soaking time — generally 30 minutes to ensure complete and uniform austenitization throughout the cross-section.

  3. 3
    Check Water Level in Jominy Apparatus

    Check the water level in the tank of the Jominy apparatus. If less, fill water from the top to ensure there is a continuous uninterrupted water supply during the quench.

  4. 4
    Adjust Water Flow Rate

    Check the free water height from the orifice. Adjust the flow rate of water so that it forms an umbrella pattern when the test piece is kept in position on the Jominy bath fixture. Standard free jet height = 63.5 mm (2.5 inches).

🔥 Operational Procedure
  1. 5
    Remove Hot Specimen from Furnace

    Remove the hot red specimen from the furnace with the help of tongs provided. Handle carefully to avoid burns and ensure transfer time to the Jominy fixture is within 5 seconds to prevent premature transformation.

  2. 6
    Place on Jominy Bath Fixture

    Keep the specimen on the fixture provided in the Jominy bath tub. The bottom end of the specimen should be 12.5 mm above the water orifice. The specimen should be held by the shoulder, allowing the water jet to impinge only on the bottom face.

  3. 7
    Turn on Pump Switch

    Turn on the pump switch so that water comes from the water supply pipe. The water jet must hit only the bottom end face of the specimen — not the sides. Ensure the umbrella spray pattern is formed.

  4. 8
    Continue Quenching for 10 Minutes

    After this, the cooling of the specimen will be completed by the test specimen spraying water at least for 10 minutes. The specimen must remain undisturbed on the fixture throughout this period.

  5. 9
    Turn Off Pump Switch

    After the 10-minute quench period is complete, turn off the pump switch. Remove the specimen carefully using tongs — it may still be hot.

📏 Ending Operations and Data Collection
  1. 10
    Grind Flat Surfaces

    Grind two diametrically opposite flat surfaces along the length of the specimen to a depth of 0.38 mm (0.015 inch) to remove any decarburized layer and expose the true microstructure for hardness testing.

  2. 11
    Calculate Hardness at Different Points

    Measure Rockwell hardness (HRC scale) at regular intervals from the quenched end — typically at 1.5 mm, 3 mm, 5 mm, 7 mm, 9 mm, 11 mm, 13 mm … up to the full length. Mark distances with a scriber and record all readings.

  3. 12
    Plot Hardenability Graph

    Plot the hardenability curve with distance from quenched end (mm) on the X-axis and Rockwell Hardness (HRC) on the Y-axis. Connect the points with a smooth curve. This is the Jominy curve for the tested steel.

📊 Steel Grades Comparison
AISI 1040 — Plain Carbon SteelLow hardenability. Hardness drops rapidly from quenched end. Suitable for small section components. Jominy J distance (J₁₀) ≈ 5–8 mm.
AISI 4140 — Chromium-Molybdenum SteelMedium-high hardenability. Cr and Mo retard transformation. Good for medium section parts (shafts, gears). J₁₀ ≈ 15–25 mm.
AISI 8620 — Nickel-Chromium-MolybdenumCase-hardening steel. Moderate core hardenability with excellent surface properties. J₁₀ ≈ 10–18 mm.
AISI 4340 — High Alloy SteelVery high hardenability. Remains hard across the full bar length. Used for large forgings, aircraft parts. J₁₀ ≈ 35–50 mm.

Assignment

Practical and written tasks to reinforce learning from Jominy test

Assignment 1 — Jominy Test Execution & Curve Plotting
Practical

Perform the Jominy end-quench test on the given steel specimen and record the hardness data.

Task A — Observation TableRecord Rockwell hardness (HRC) readings at distances of 1.5, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50 mm from the quenched end. Present data in a tabular format.
Task B — Hardenability CurvePlot the Jominy curve (HRC vs Distance from quenched end in mm) on graph paper. Label the axes, mark the data points, and draw a smooth best-fit curve. Identify the J-distance (the distance at which HRC drops to a specified value, e.g. HRC 40).
Task C — Hardness BandSuperimpose the measured curve on the standard hardenability band for the steel grade used. Comment on whether the specimen falls within the acceptable band, and what this implies about the batch quality.
Assignment 2 — Comparative Hardenability Study
Written Report

Prepare a written report (minimum 5 pages) on the comparative hardenability of different steels:

Part 1 — Alloying Element AnalysisExplain how each of the following alloying elements affects the hardenability of steel, and why: Carbon (C), Manganese (Mn), Chromium (Cr), Molybdenum (Mo), Nickel (Ni), Boron (B). Use the Grossmann hardenability concept to support your answer.
Part 2 — Jominy Curves ComparisonSketch (or plot from data) the Jominy curves for AISI 1020, 1040, 4140, and 4340 steels on the same axes. Discuss the shape differences and what they imply about the depth of hardening in a 50 mm diameter bar of each grade.
Part 3 — Industrial ApplicationSelect one industrial application (gear, shaft, die, or structural member) and justify the steel grade selection based on hardenability requirements, section size, and service conditions. Include cooling rate calculations if applicable.
Assignment 3 — Viva Preparation & Calculations
Viva + Numerical

Solve the following numerical problems and prepare for viva questions:

Problem 1A steel bar of diameter 40 mm is quenched in water. Using the Jominy curve for AISI 4140, determine the hardness at the surface and at the centre of the bar. Use the Lamont chart for cooling rate correlation.
Problem 2Explain why two steels with the same carbon content can have different hardenabilities. Calculate the Ideal Critical Diameter (DI) for a 0.4%C steel with 0.8%Mn, 0.5%Cr, 0.2%Mo using the multiplying factor method.
Problem 3A Jominy specimen showed HRC values of 58, 55, 50, 44, 36, 30, 28, 27 at distances 1.5, 3, 5, 7, 10, 13, 16, 20 mm. Calculate the hardness drop rate (HRC/mm) and classify the steel as low, medium, or high hardenability.
